# Visual storytelling (D01)

The camera decides what the viewer knows, feels and remembers, and when. Every technique in this skill serves one of those three jobs. Cinematography comes from the Greek for "writing with motion" (S35); like a painter who must tell the whole story in one frame, nothing in the frame should be accidental - every element, colour and shadow has a purpose (S35).

## The information ledger
For each beat write two columns: KNOWS / MUST NOT KNOW YET.
- **Suspense** = the viewer knows more than the character (show the danger first, then the person walking into it).
- **Surprise** = the viewer knows less (hide the cause until the reveal).
- **Mystery** = the viewer knows a question exists, not the answer (show the effect, hide the cause).
Choose one per sequence on purpose. Suspense lasts; surprise is spent in one frame.

## See / hide list
| Hidden thing | Hide method | Reveal at | Reveal method |
|---|---|---|---|
Hide methods: frame edge (out of shot), foreground object (blocking), shallow focus, darkness / silhouette, angle (behind the subject), distance (too far to read), time (cut away before it is seen).
Reveal methods: camera move (pan, pull-out, arc, crane), subject move into frame, focus pull, light change, a cut to a wider or closer size.
**Rule:** a reveal needs a hide. Write them as a pair. A reveal with no prior concealment is only a shot.

## Abstract narration -> concrete pictures (5 routes)
| Route | What you show | Best for | Example ("data leaks") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literal | the thing itself | facts, objects | the leaked file on a screen |
| Process | the thing working, step by step | explaining | packets moving from server to an unknown machine |
| Metaphor | a physical stand-in | remembering, feeling | water dripping from a sealed vault |
| Consequence | what changes because of it | stakes | a bank alert on a phone at night |
| Human | a person reacting | empathy | a face lit by the alert, then the hand stops |
Choose by the beat's job: explain -> literal/process; feel -> human/consequence; remember -> metaphor that comes back later. A metaphor must be readable without narration in the shot's duration; if it needs the voice to decode it, use literal.

## Show versus tell
Do not illustrate every word ("show and tell"). Let the picture carry what the words do not: if narration says "she was afraid", show the hand on the door handle that does not turn. Under dense explanation, the picture supports (literal/process) and the camera is still.

## Visual hierarchy and attention
The eye goes to: faces and eyes, text, the brightest area, the sharpest area, the highest contrast, movement, the largest or nearest shape, isolated shapes. Design each frame so the primary subject wins at least three of these. One primary subject per frame. When attention must move, move it with one lever at a time (focus, light, movement, or a cut).

## Subjective and objective
- Subjective (we are with a character): POV, over-the-shoulder, close sizes, eye-level or the character's height, handheld or follow moves. Use when the viewer must share an experience.
- Objective (we observe): wider, static or slow, side or high angles, longer lens from a distance. Use when the viewer must understand or judge a situation.
- Switch between them on purpose, usually at a turn.

## Contrast and callbacks
- Visual contrast creates meaning: big/small, light/dark, still/moving, order/chaos, warm/cool. Put contrasts on the cut at a turn.
- Callback: repeat a frame, angle, motif or colour from earlier with one change. The change carries the meaning (the empty chair from the opening, now occupied). Record callbacks in the motif register.

## Visual identity
Decide once per video and hold it: perspective (typical lens/distance), camera behaviour (still / floating / handheld), palette, light quality, aspect ratio, typical composition. Change the identity only at a story turn, and make the change visible (Oppenheimer's colour vs black-and-white strands; Dune's infrared world look - `references/cinematographers.md`).

## Storytelling without dialogue
Use: blocking (who is near whom, who faces whom), eyelines, objects with meaning, light changes, camera distance changes, repetition with change, and duration (a held shot says "this matters").

## Common mistakes
- Shots that decorate instead of inform.
- Reveals with no set-up.
- A new metaphor for every sentence.
- Close-ups everywhere: no emphasis is left for the peak.
- The camera moving while the viewer must read.
