# Composition and frame design (D06)

Composition is chosen for meaning, then checked for hierarchy. Rules are tools; the LibreTexts handbook notes that rules are broken deliberately once mastered (S17).

## Choose the organising principle by meaning
| Principle | Meaning it carries |
|---|---|
| Centre / symmetry | order, authority, confrontation, ritual, a world under control; also unease when perfect symmetry is broken |
| Rule of thirds | natural, conversational, a subject in a living space |
| Asymmetry + negative space | isolation, anticipation, a threat in the empty part, room for text |
| Frame within a frame | confinement, voyeurism, focus, a character trapped by place |
| Leading lines | direction of attention, travel, destiny |
| Geometric shapes (triangles, circles, grids) | stability (triangle base), unity (circle), system/bureaucracy (grid) |
| Golden ratio / spiral | a natural flow of the eye in a busy frame; use as a check, not a target |

## Techniques

### Rule of thirds
What: divide the frame in three both ways; place subjects on lines or crossings (S17). Effect: balanced but alive.
Use when: a natural, unforced look. Avoid when: applied mechanically; symmetry or centre carries a stronger meaning. Source: S17.

### Golden ratio and golden spiral
What: a 1:1.618 division and its spiral. Effect: similar to thirds with a slightly offset balance point.
Use when: checking a complex frame's flow. Avoid when: claiming it as a law; viewers do not measure it. Source: craft convention.

### Symmetry and centre composition
What: the subject on the vertical centre; mirrored halves. Effect: authority, confrontation, formality, an unnatural or controlled world.
Use when: a presenter addresses the viewer; a confrontation; a ritual. In 9:16, centre is often the strongest default. Avoid when: a natural conversation. Source: craft convention.

### Asymmetry
What: unequal weight balanced by space or a secondary element. Effect: tension, movement, life.
Use when: dynamic scenes; a subject with something unseen in the empty side. Avoid when: the frame feels merely unfinished. Source: craft convention.

### Leading lines
What: lines (roads, rails, edges, light) that point to the subject. Effect: the eye travels to the subject; a sense of path.
Use when: wide shots, reveals, journeys. Avoid when: lines lead out of the frame or to nothing. Source: craft convention.

### Negative and positive space
What: positive = subject mass; negative = empty area. Effect: negative space gives isolation, breath, anticipation, and room for text.
Use when: isolation beats; text placement (explainers); suspense (something may enter). Avoid when: the empty part has no meaning and the subject is too small to read. Source: craft convention.

### Frame within a frame
What: a door, window, screen, mirror or arch frames the subject. Effect: confinement, observation, focus.
Use when: a character trapped or watched; screens in tech stories. Avoid when: the inner frame is too small to read in 9:16. Source: craft convention.

### Headroom
What: space above the head (S17). Effect: too much = small, weak; too little = cramped (sometimes intended in BCU).
Use when: CU/MCU: eyes about one third from the top. Avoid when: -. Mistake: same headroom for different sizes. Source: S17.

### Lead room / looking room (nose room)
What: space in front of a moving subject or in the direction of the gaze (S17). Effect: natural flow; denying it creates tension or entrapment.
Use when: always as default. Avoid when: the character is blocked, pursued or trapped - then deny it on purpose. Source: S17.

### Visual balance and visual weight
What: weight comes from size, brightness, contrast, saturation, faces, text, isolation and position. Effect: a balanced frame feels stable; an unbalanced one, tense.
Use when: check every key frame: does the primary subject win at least three attention cues? Avoid when: -. Source: craft convention.

### Foreground, middle ground, background (layering)
What: elements at three depths; overlap creates depth on a flat screen (S17). Effect: a real, inhabited space; depth for camera moves (parallax).
Use when: the space matters; before a truck or push (layers make the move readable). Avoid when: explainer graphics that must read flat and clean. Source: S17.

### Edge awareness
What: control of what touches or crosses the frame edges. Effect: clean frames; tension when something cuts in from the edge.
Use when: always: no limbs cut at joints, no bright objects pulling the eye to the edge, no content in platform UI zones. Avoid when: -. Source: craft convention; S29.

### Dynamic framing (composition over time)
What: the composition changes inside the shot (subject moves, camera moves, focus changes). Effect: the frame tells a small story.
Use when: write the shot as START frame -> END frame with subject positions for both. Avoid when: the end frame is not designed. Source: craft convention.

### Aspect ratio as the first frame decision
What: the frame's width to height (1.33 academy, 1.78 HD, 2.35-2.39 scope, 9:16 vertical) chosen before composing (S19). Effect: wide frames hold landscapes and groups and isolate a single face; narrow frames favour single figures and vertical shapes.
Use when: decide once per video (or per strand, as Oppenheimer's formats do - S13). Avoid when: mixing ratios without a story reason. Source: S19, S13.

### Relative size and placement as power
What: within one frame, the larger or higher figure dominates; equal sizes on the thirds read as equals (S19). Hitchcock's often-quoted rule (listed in Brown's contents, S36): the size of an object in the frame should match its importance to the story at that moment. Effect: power relations without dialogue.
Use when: status changes inside a scene; key objects (a clue gets size). Avoid when: accidental size differences that suggest a power relation you do not mean. Source: S19, S36.

## Vertical 9:16 frame
- Use the user's house values when a rulebook exists (S29: caption band, presenter zone, content at screen y <= 1320 in split scenes, camera scale <= 1.0-1.05 when content touches the margins).
- Without house values: keep faces, text and key details inside the central 4:5 area and leave clear space at the bottom for platform UI (third-party guides disagree on exact margins - S26; label them as conventions).
- Prefer centre and vertical stacking (subject above, information below); wide shots need a vertical shape.

## Mistakes
- Mechanical thirds on every shot.
- Text over a face or a key diagram.
- No designed end frame for a moving shot.
- Layers that confuse the hierarchy.
