# Shot types, angles and camera position (D02, D03)

Core rule: **shot size = emotional distance x information need.** Wide when the relation between subject and place is the information. Close when a face or detail is the information. Change at least one size step (or >= 30° of angle) between consecutive shots of the same subject, or the cut reads as a jump cut (S31).

Vertical 9:16 note: the frame is narrow, so sizes read one step closer than in 16:9. A 9:16 MCU of a presenter is the "wide" of a talking-head Short; a true wide shot needs a strong vertical subject (tower, stairs, a standing figure).

Sources: shot vocabulary S03, S16, S17, S20; jump-cut / 30° S31; character relation S09. Entries marked "craft convention" are standard film-school usage, not a single source.

## Shot sizes

### Extreme wide shot (EWS)
What: the place dominates; people are small or absent. Effect: scale, isolation, awe, "where are we".
Use when: a new world or place matters; to show a person is small against a force. Avoid when: the viewer must read a face or detail; in 9:16 without a strong vertical shape.
Composition: one clear shape or path for the eye; horizon placed for meaning (low = sky/hope/threat; high = land/burden). Mistake: a pretty landscape with no story job. Source: craft convention.

### Establishing shot
What: the first view of a location in a scene (often EWS or WS). Effect: orientation - the viewer knows where and when.
Use when: the location changes and geography matters later. Avoid when: the place does not matter, or the story wants disorientation (start close, reveal later).
Mistake: establishing a place the scene never uses. Source: craft convention; S20.

### Wide shot (WS) / long shot
What: full body with a lot of environment. Effect: relation of person and place; blocking reads.
Use when: action covers space; two or more people move; comedy of the body. Avoid when: emotion lives in the eyes. Source: S03.

### Full shot (FS)
What: head to feet fills the frame. Effect: body language, costume, stance.
Use when: posture or movement tells the story (dance, fight, a slump). Avoid when: the face carries the beat. Source: S03.

### Medium-wide shot (MWS / cowboy)
What: knees or mid-thigh up. Effect: a person with gesture plus some setting.
Use when: walk-and-talk, presenter with props, two people standing. Avoid when: intimacy is needed. Source: craft convention.

### Medium shot (MS)
What: waist up. Effect: neutral, conversational, social distance.
Use when: dialogue, presenters, explaining with hands. Avoid when: you need the peak emotion (save closer sizes). Source: S03.

### Medium close-up (MCU)
What: chest up. Effect: personal attention, still some body language.
Use when: the main size for narration to camera and interviews; in 9:16 the default presenter size. Avoid when: the hands or the setting carry information. Source: craft convention.

### Close-up (CU)
What: the face fills most of the frame. Effect: intimacy, emotion, emphasis.
Use when: a reaction, a decision, a realisation. Avoid when: used on every shot (no emphasis left); before the viewer knows who the person is.
Composition: eyes about one third from the top; looking room in the gaze direction. Source: S03.

### Big close-up (BCU / choker)
What: forehead to chin. Effect: intense, nowhere to hide.
Use when: the peak of tension, a lie, a breaking point. Avoid when: the moment is mild; with a wide lens very close (distortion) unless distortion is the point. Source: craft convention.

### Extreme close-up (ECU)
What: an eye, a mouth, a key, a cursor. Effect: the detail is the whole world for a moment.
Use when: a clue, a decisive action (finger on the button), a texture that matters. Avoid when: the viewer does not yet know what the object is (show it in context first). Source: S03.

### Over-the-shoulder (OTS)
What: from behind one person's shoulder towards another person or a screen. Effect: relation, point of view of a listener, confrontation.
Use when: dialogue coverage; a person looking at a screen the viewer must read. Avoid when: the shoulder hides the key information; crossing the line between reverse shots. Source: S03; continuity S17.

### Point-of-view (POV)
What: the camera sees what the character sees. Effect: subjectivity, identification, suspense.
Use when: we must share perception (a search, fear, a discovery). Avoid when: we need to see the character's reaction (follow the POV with a reaction shot). Execution: physical - lens near the eye height of the character, handheld if they move; AI - "first-person view"; virtual - parent the camera to the head bone. Source: S03.

### Two-shot
What: two people in one frame. Effect: relationship; distance between them = emotional distance.
Use when: a relationship changes; staging can show the change (closer, apart, one turns away). Avoid when: one person's reaction is the story. Source: craft convention.

### Profile shot
What: the subject seen from the side. Effect: observation, determination, travel, or confrontation (two profiles facing).
Use when: a person moves through space; two people face off; a character should feel a little mysterious (S19). Avoid when: you need the eyes. Source: S03, S19.

### Insert shot
What: a close view of an object or action within the scene. Effect: information the viewer must not miss.
Use when: a detail is a clue or a step in a process. Avoid when: it only fills time. Source: craft convention.

### Cutaway
What: a shot of something outside the main action (another place, a related object). Effect: context, time compression, parallel meaning.
Use when: hiding an edit in an interview, showing what the speaker talks about, compressing time. Avoid when: it breaks tension that must hold. Source: craft convention.

### Reaction shot
What: a person responding to something. Effect: tells the viewer how to feel; often stronger than the action.
Use when: the meaning of an event is in its effect on someone. Avoid when: the reaction is obvious and slows the pace. Source: craft convention; S21 (emotion first).

### Master shot
What: a wide shot that covers the whole scene action. Effect: geography; a safe base for editing.
Use when: complex blocking; return to it when geography changes or the viewer may be lost. Avoid when: the scene is about one face. Source: craft convention.

### Tracking shot (as a shot type)
What: the camera travels with a moving subject. Effect: continuity of experience, momentum.
Use when: the journey is the story (1917 - S09). Avoid when: the subject does not move, or the move hides information. Movement detail: `camera/movement_and_zoom.md`. Source: S09.

### Reveal shot
What: a shot designed to bring hidden information into view (move, focus, light or subject entry). Effect: surprise, discovery, or the release of suspense.
Use when: the see/hide list has a reveal here. Avoid when: there was no hide. Source: craft convention; `core/visual_storytelling.md`.

## Angles and camera height

Default: eye level of the subject, three-quarter view. Change it only for a reason.

### Eye level
What: lens at the subject's eye height. Effect: equality, neutrality, honesty.
Use when: presenters, interviews, peers. Avoid when: a power relation must be felt. Source: S03.

### Low angle
What: lens below the eyes, looking up. Effect: power, threat, heroism, awe; also instability if extreme.
Use when: the story gives the subject power at this moment. Avoid when: on a narrator meant to be a friend; to look "cool". Source: S03, S41, S42.

### High angle
What: lens above, looking down. Effect: vulnerability, smallness, overview.
Use when: a character loses control; to show a layout. Avoid when: the subject must look strong. Source: S03, S41 (small, weak, sometimes lonely).

### Bird's-eye / overhead / top-down
What: straight down. Effect: pattern, map, process, fate; removes depth.
Use when: desks, hands at work, board games, processes, crowds as patterns; explainers. Avoid when: faces matter. Source: S03.

### Worm's-eye / ground level
What: lens at or near the ground. Effect: extreme scale, the world from below, or a child's/animal's view.
Use when: footsteps, a falling object, monumental scale. Avoid when: used to decorate a calm scene. Source: S03.

### Dutch angle (canted)
What: the horizon is tilted. Effect: unease, a world that is wrong.
Use when: the character's world is disordered (drugs, madness, crisis) - sparingly. Avoid when: for "energy"; in explainers. Source: S03, S41.

### Frontal
What: the subject faces the lens. Effect: address, confrontation, symmetry, authority.
Use when: talking to camera; a formal or confrontational beat. Avoid when: natural conversation (feels staged). Source: craft convention.

### Three-quarter
What: the face turned about 45° from the lens. Effect: shape and eyes together; the standard flattering angle.
Use when: most dialogue and interview coverage. Avoid when: a formal address or a profile meaning is needed. Source: craft convention.

### Rear angle
What: from behind the subject. Effect: mystery, following, what is ahead matters more than the face.
Use when: the subject enters the unknown, or what is ahead is important (S09: avoid long rear views unless something matters ahead). Avoid when: emotion is the beat. Source: S09.

### Shoulder level
What: lens at shoulder height, slightly below the eyes. Effect: slightly heroic but natural; common in action and westerns.
Use when: a confident character, mild strength. Avoid when: strict neutrality is needed. Source: craft convention.

## Camera distance (position vs size)
Size can come from distance or focal length. They are not the same: a closer camera changes perspective (bigger nose, faster falloff of the background); a longer lens from far keeps the perspective of the far position (`camera/lens_language.md`, V2). Decide the distance for the relationship (presence = near; observation = far), then the lens for the size.
